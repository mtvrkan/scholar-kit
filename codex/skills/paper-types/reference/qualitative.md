# Qualitative Research Studies

## COREQ & SRQR Essentials

Two main checklists for qualitative research:

- **COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research):** 32 items for interview/focus group studies.
- **SRQR (Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research):** 21 items, broader (includes ethnography, observation, document analysis).

Both map to: Personal characteristics (researcher) → Design (what was studied, why, how?) → Recruitment/sampling → Data collection → Data analysis → Findings → Reflexivity & trustworthiness.

### Key items across both

| Aspect | What to Report | Typical Location |
|--------|---|---|
| Research question | Explicit, stated upfront (not: "We explored…" vaguely) | Introduction |
| Study design | Qualitative design type (phenomenology, grounded theory, ethnography, thematic analysis, etc.) | Methods: Design |
| Theoretical framework | Underlying theory/lens (if any) | Introduction or Methods |
| Researcher positionality & reflexivity | Who did the research? What are their assumptions, background, potential biases? | Methods or Discussion |
| Participants: recruitment | How were participants found and approached? | Methods: Recruitment |
| Participants: characteristics | N, age range, gender, role/occupation, other relevant demographics (table or narrative) | Methods or Results |
| Participants: informed consent | How was informed consent obtained? Any special protections? | Methods or ethics statement |
| Data collection procedures | Interview/focus group protocol (semi-structured, open-ended); who conducted; duration; setting; audio-recorded? | Methods: Data Collection |
| Interview/FG topic guide | Guide questions provided (in appendix or main text) | Appendix or Methods |
| Data management | How were data managed (transcription, anonymization, storage, security)? | Methods |
| Data analysis procedure | Analysis method & software; coding process (deductive, inductive, iterative); codebook development; theme identification | Methods: Data Analysis |
| Quality assurance | Measures to ensure rigor (member checking, peer debriefing, triangulation, negative case analysis, saturation criterion) | Methods |
| Findings presentation | Themes with supporting quotes, page numbers; narrative coherence | Results |
| Conflict/divergent findings | How were contrary or conflicting findings handled? | Results or Discussion |
| Trustworthiness claim | Credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability (or Lincoln & Guba criteria equivalent) | Methods or Discussion |

## Theme–Quote–Source Chain Discipline (CRITICAL)

This is the hallmark of rigorous qualitative work and the #1 area for errors.

### Rule: Every Quote Must Have

1. **Verbatim text** — exact words from participant, in quotation marks.
2. **Source identification** — Participant ID or initials + pseudonym if used (e.g., "P03_Sarah" or "Participant 12").
3. **Page or timestamp** — Page in transcript, or timestamp in audio transcript (crucial for verification and for readers to locate the passage).
4. **Explanatory context** — 1–2 sentences before the quote explaining who this person is and why this quote matters.

### Format example

> "I felt frustrated because nobody asked me what I wanted to do" (P07_Alex, p. 4). This frustration appeared across multiple interviews [CONTINUE with interpretation].

Or for multiple quotes supporting one theme:

> **Theme: Lack of agency**  
> Participants described feeling excluded from decision-making. One participant stated: "I felt frustrated because nobody asked me what I wanted to do" (P07_Alex, p. 4). Another echoed: "They decided for me without even asking" (P12_Jordan, p. 8). This sense of powerlessness shaped participants' overall experience.

### Anti-patterns (AVOID)

| Bad | Why It's Bad | Good |
|-----|------|-----|
| "Participants felt frustrated" (no quote) | Unsupported claim without evidence | Quote the participant or cite the analysis step |
| "P07 said 'frustration'" (page unknown) | Readers can't verify; unreproducible | Include page: "P07, p. 4" |
| "People were frustrated [CITATION NEEDED]" | Orphan claim, not tied to your data | Only quote/cite when you analyzed this participant |
| "As one participant said: [quote]" (no ID) | Can't track back to source | Always include participant ID: "P07_Alex" |
| Long quote (>100 words, not marked as block quote) | Disrupts flow; unclear which part you're analyzing | Extract the key sentence; use ellipsis (…) or block quote format if long |
| Quote from field notes, not transcribed interview | Ambiguous source | Clarify: "In field notes following the interview (P07, field note p. 2)…" |

## Saturation Criterion

**Definition:** Saturation is reached when additional data collection and analysis no longer yield new themes or insights; you're seeing repetition of existing themes.

**Reporting saturation:**
- "Interviews were continued until thematic saturation was reached (n = 23 participants)."
- Or: "We conducted 5 focus groups (20 participants total) and found that by the 5th group, no new themes emerged, indicating saturation."
- Document: How many participants? How many interviews/FGs? When did you determine saturation?

**Why it matters:** Saturation justifies your sample size (N is not fixed in qualitative research; it's determined by depth of understanding, not statistical power).

## Data Analysis Procedure Template

### Thematic Analysis (most common example)

```
Step 1: Transcription and familiarization
- Audio interviews transcribed verbatim (by XYZ, checked against audio by ABC).
- Researchers read all transcripts twice, noting initial impressions.

Step 2: Open coding
- Each transcript coded line-by-line by 2 independent coders (A and B).
- Codes assigned inductively (not pre-determined; emerged from data).
- Discrepancies discussed until consensus reached.
- Initial N = 47 codes.

Step 3: Theme development
- Codes were grouped by similarity into candidate themes.
- Themes were reviewed against original data to ensure fidelity.
- Final N = 7 themes (list them).

Step 4: Refinement and validation
- Member checking: 3 participants reviewed findings; all confirmed themes were accurate.
- Peer debriefing: research team discussed findings and alternative interpretations.

Step 5: Final thematic structure
- Themes named and described; quotes selected per theme to illustrate.
- Analysis conducted using QDA software [name and version].
```

### Key elements to document

- **Coding approach:** Deductive (codes pre-set from theory) vs. inductive (codes emerged from data) vs. hybrid.
- **Multiple coders:** Use 2+ to check reliability; report inter-coder agreement (e.g., Cohen's κ if quantified).
- **Constant comparison:** Did you compare new data to existing codes, refining the codebook iteratively?
- **Negative cases:** Instances that didn't fit the theme—how were they explained?
- **Member checking:** Did participants review findings? What did they change or confirm?

## Trustworthiness Strategies

| Lincoln & Guba Criterion | Strategy | How to Report |
|---|---|---|
| **Credibility** (truth value—do findings fit reality?) | Member checking; prolonged engagement; triangulation; peer debriefing | "Transcripts were member-checked with [N] participants; all confirmed the themes were accurate." |
| **Transferability** (applicability to other contexts?) | Thick description; transparent sampling; context documentation | "We provide detailed descriptions of participant characteristics and setting so readers can assess applicability." |
| **Dependability** (consistency—would another researcher find similar themes?) | Audit trail; codebook documentation; reflexivity | "A detailed audit trail documenting all analysis decisions is available upon request." |
| **Confirmability** (neutrality—are findings grounded in data, not researcher bias?) | Bracketing; reflexivity; attention to alternative interpretations | "Researchers acknowledged [potential bias X] and actively sought disconfirming evidence." |

## Reflexivity Statement

**Why it matters:** Qualitative research is interpretive; the researcher is part of the instrument. Readers need to know: Who are you? What might bias your interpretation?

**Template:**
```
The research team comprised [brief description: A is a licensed therapist with 10 years clinical experience in [topic], 
which gave her deep understanding but also potential bias toward seeing [X]. B is a newcomer to this field with no 
clinical background, bringing an outsider perspective. We discussed these differences during analysis to triangulate 
interpretations and mitigate individual biases.
```

**Include:**
- Professional background & credentials.
- Personal connection to topic (if any).
- Assumptions you brought (e.g., "I initially believed that X was the main driver, but data surprised me").
- How you managed bias (explicit discussion during coding, seeking disconfirming evidence, etc.).

## Results Presentation for Qualitative

### Structure

1. **Opening paragraph:** Broad overview of the phenomenon studied and main themes.
2. **One section per major theme:** 
   - Theme name + definition (1 sentence).
   - Description of the theme (2–3 sentences).
   - 2–3 representative quotes (with participant IDs and page numbers).
   - Explanation of how the quotes illustrate the theme.
3. **Divergent or conflicting findings** (if applicable):
   - Participants who didn't fit the theme; alternative perspectives.
   - Acknowledge the variability; don't homogenize.
4. **Contextual factors** (if relevant):
   - Did demographics, timing, or context affect themes?
   - Subgroup differences (if any).

### Figure/Table for qualitative findings

- **Thematic map:** Visual representation showing themes and sub-themes (tree diagram or concept map).
- **Participant characteristics table:** Demographic summary (N, age range, gender, occupation, etc.).
- **Quotes table** (optional): Organized by theme with participant ID and page number for quick reference (useful for readers).

## Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Themes not grounded in data (researcher imposed theory) | Quote multiple participants per theme; ensure theme emerged from coding, not pre-assumed |
| Quotes without page numbers (unreproducible) | Always include timestamp or page; if oral interview, note duration marker (e.g., "23:15–23:45" in audio) |
| Orphan themes (1–2 quotes, then vanish) | If a theme doesn't have ≥3 strong supporting quotes, question whether it's a true theme or an outlier |
| No discussion of disconfirming evidence (glossing over contradictions) | Explicitly state: "Three participants disagreed; they believed [alternative]. We explored why…" |
| Pseudonyms used inconsistently (P01, Participant 1, "one woman," etc.) | Choose a system; stick to it throughout (e.g., all "P##" or all names) |
| Saturation not discussed (reader doesn't know if N=8 was enough) | State: "Interviews continued to saturation (n=16); by interview 14–16 no new themes emerged" |
| No reflexivity statement (reader doesn't know researcher background) | Include 2–3 sentences on who conducted the research and potential biases |
| Findings presented as facts, not interpretations ("Participants ARE frustrated") | Use hedging: "Participants DESCRIBED themselves as frustrated"; "Frustration EMERGED as a key theme" |
