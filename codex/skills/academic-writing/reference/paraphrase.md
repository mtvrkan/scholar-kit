# Paraphrase Discipline: Source-Faithful Rewriting

## Core Principle: Structure Change Required

**Paraphrasing is NOT picking synonyms.** It's rewriting an idea in your own structure while preserving the original meaning and citing the source.

### The Three-Part Transformation

1. **Keep the core idea** (don't change the meaning).
2. **Change the sentence structure** (not just swap words).
3. **Always cite** (even paraphrase needs attribution).

### Example

**Original (from source):**
> "Deep neural networks achieve superior performance on image classification tasks because they learn hierarchical representations of visual features." (Smith et al., 2020)

**Bad Paraphrase (synonyms only; still plagiarism):**
> "Deep neural networks get better results on image sorting work since they acquire hierarchical depictions of visual attributes." — PLAGIARISM (same structure, synonyms swapped)

**Good Paraphrase (structure changed; cited):**
> "The hierarchical feature learning enabled by deep neural networks explains their superior classification performance on visual recognition tasks" [@smith2020]. — PARAPHRASE ✓

**Quote (when original wording is essential):**
> Smith et al. (2020) argue that deep neural networks succeed in image classification because they "learn hierarchical representations of visual features" (p. 42). — QUOTE ✓

---

## Three Levels of Borrowing (Know Which You're Doing)

| Level | What You're Doing | Citation Needed? | Format |
|-------|------------------|---|---|
| **Paraphrase** | Rewriting the idea in your own words + structure | YES | Normal citation [@author2020] or Author (year) |
| **Quotation** | Using the author's exact words | YES (+ page #) | "Quotation" (p. XX) or (Author, p. XX) |
| **Summary** | Condensing multiple ideas from a source | YES | Normal citation [@author2020] |

**CRITICAL:** All three require citations. The only thing that doesn't require citation is common knowledge (e.g., "The Earth orbits the Sun").

## Paraphrase Technique: Step-by-Step

### Step 1: Read the Original (Multiple Times)

- Read the full sentence/paragraph once.
- Put it away.
- Read it again.
- Understand the core idea without looking at specific words.

**Wrong approach:** Glancing once, then trying to rewrite while looking at the text. You'll just swap synonyms → plagiarism.

### Step 2: Identify the Core Idea(s)

**Original:**
> "Convolutional neural networks achieve state-of-the-art performance on image classification because they exploit spatial locality through local filters and weight sharing."

**Core ideas:**
1. CNNs are top performers at image classification.
2. They use local filters.
3. They use weight sharing.
4. These features exploit spatial structure in images.

### Step 3: Rewrite in Your Own Structure

- Use your own words.
- Reorder concepts (don't preserve paragraph order).
- Vary sentence type (complex → simple, active → passive, etc.).

**Rewrite (Structure changed):**
> "The state-of-the-art performance of CNNs in image classification stems from two architectural features: weight sharing (which enables efficient learning across image regions) and local filtering (which captures spatial patterns). Together, these mechanisms exploit the inherent spatial structure of image data." [@author2020]

**Compare:**
- Original: "X exploit Y through A and B"
- Paraphrase: "X stems from A and B, which exploit Y"
- Structure is different ✓

### Step 4: Self-Check (Before Finalizing)

Use these questions to verify your paraphrase is not plagiarism:

| Question | Good Answer | Bad Answer |
|----------|---|---|
| Can I read the original and my paraphrase side-by-side and see obvious restructuring? | YES (structures differ visibly) | NO (looks like synonyms swapped) |
| Did I use any 3+ consecutive words from the original without quotation marks? | NO (or they're common phrases) | YES (indicates copying) |
| Can I explain the core idea in my own words without the source in front of me? | YES (I understand it) | NO (I'm just mimicking structure) |
| Did I cite the source? | YES | NO ❌ |

## When to Quote (Instead of Paraphrasing)

Quote when the **original wording is essential** to your argument. Examples:

### 1. Definition or Key Term
**Quote when:** The author coins or precisely defines a term.
> "Smith et al. (2020) define 'transfer learning' as 'the practice of using parameters learned on a large dataset to initialize training on a smaller, task-specific dataset' (p. 15)." 

(Paraphrase would lose precision.)

### 2. Important Methodology or Finding
**Quote when:** The specific wording captures a crucial point.
> "The study found that 'participants shown diverse perspectives were 3x more likely to change their opinion' (p. 42), suggesting that perspective diversity is a key driver of belief change."

(Direct quote highlights the quantitative finding.)

### 3. Argument or Claim You'll Debate
**Quote when:** You'll directly respond to the author's specific phrasing.
> "Smith claims that 'deep learning is inherently interpretable' (p. 30). However, recent work shows…"

(Quoting lets you address the exact claim.)

### Rule of Thumb

- **Paraphrase:** 80–90% of citations (unless wording is essential).
- **Quote:** 10–20% of citations (key definitions, findings, phrases you'll debate).

## Quote Format (Parenthetical vs. Narrative)

### Parenthetical Citation (when quote is not part of a sentence)

> "As one researcher noted, 'deep learning has transformed computer vision' (Smith, 2020, p. 42)."

**Format:** [Full sentence with quote] ([Author, Year, p. XX].)

### Narrative Citation (when you introduce the author in the sentence)

> "Smith (2020) argues that 'deep learning has transformed computer vision' (p. 42)."

**Format:** [Author (Year)] [quote] ([p. XX].)

### APA 7 Example (Multiple Citations)

**Paraphrase:**
> Neural networks require careful tuning of hyperparameters to avoid overfitting [@smith2020; @jones2021].

**Quote:**
> Smith (2020) notes that "hyperparameter tuning is the art of balancing accuracy and generalization" (p. 30), while Jones (2021) argues that this challenge has been simplified by automated search methods [@jones2021, p. 45].

## Common Paraphrase Mistakes (Avoid These)

### Mistake 1: Synonym Substitution (Still Plagiarism)

| Original | Bad "Paraphrase" | Why It's Wrong |
|---|---|---|
| "Deep learning models excel at image classification." | "Deep learning models are great at image categorization." | Just synonyms (excel→great, classification→categorization). Same structure. |
| "The algorithm learns features through backpropagation." | "The algorithm gains features using backpropagation." | Only changed "learns" to "gains." Structure identical. |

**Fix:** Change structure too. "The backpropagation algorithm enables feature learning through iterative weight updates."

### Mistake 2: No Citation

> "Paraphrasing an idea from a source without citing it is plagiarism, whether or not you changed the words."

**Example (Plagiarism):**
> "Neural networks achieve superior performance on image classification tasks because they learn hierarchical representations." (Smith et al. 2020 said this; you didn't cite it.) ❌

**Example (Correct):**
> "The hierarchical feature learning in neural networks explains their superior classification performance [@smith2020]." ✓

### Mistake 3: Changing Only Words, Not Structure

| Original | Your "Paraphrase" | Issue |
|---|---|---|
| "The method achieves 95% accuracy, which is 5% better than prior work." | "Our technique attains 95% accuracy, a 5% improvement compared to previous research." | Word-for-word structure; just synonym swaps. |

**Better paraphrase:** "Compared to prior approaches, our technique demonstrates a 5-percentage-point advantage, reaching 95% accuracy [@source]."

### Mistake 4: Partial Quote Without Markers

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| "The algorithm learns hierarchical representations through backpropagation and thus achieves superior performance." (from source; mixing quote with paraphrase) | "The algorithm 'learns hierarchical representations through backpropagation' (Smith, 2020, p. 42), which contributes to its superior performance." |

**Rule:** If you take 3+ consecutive words from the source, mark them with quotation marks, even in a paraphrase.

## Integrating Citations into Your Writing

### Avoid Orphan Citations

**Bad:**
> "Neural networks learn features through backpropagation [@smith2020]. This is important for accuracy."

(Citation attached only to first sentence; second sentence is unsourced idea, not yours, and not cited.)

**Good:**
> "Neural networks learn features through backpropagation [@smith2020]. Because this mechanism enables efficient learning, accuracy improves [@smith2020]."

Or: Make clear that the second sentence is your inference:

> "Neural networks learn features through backpropagation [@smith2020]. This mechanism likely improves accuracy by enabling efficient gradient descent."

### Citing Multiple Sources Supporting One Idea

> "Transfer learning improves performance on small datasets [@smith2020; @jones2021; @patel2022]."

Or (narrative):

> "Multiple studies demonstrate that transfer learning improves small-dataset performance (Smith, 2020; Jones, 2021; Patel, 2022)."

### Citing a Source You Haven't Read (Secondary Citation)

If you read Smith's paper citing Jones's original work:

**Correct format (APA 7):**
> "Jones argued that… (as cited in Smith, 2020, p. 42)."

Or: Go read the original if possible (preferred).

## Plagiarism Checklist (Before Submitting)

- [ ] Every paraphrase has a citation attached (not just direct quotes).
- [ ] No 3+ consecutive words from source without quotation marks.
- [ ] Direct quotes are marked with quotation marks and page numbers.
- [ ] Sentences are restructured (not just synonyms swapped).
- [ ] I can explain each idea in my own words without the source in front of me.
- [ ] All ideas that are not common knowledge or my own original analysis are cited.

## Practice Exercises

### Exercise 1: Identify the Problem

**Original:** "Machine learning models can overfit to training data, resulting in poor generalization to new examples." (Chen, 2020, p. 30)

**Your text:** "Machine learning models may overfit on training data, leading to poor generalization on novel examples." [@chen2020]

**Question:** Is this plagiarism?

**Answer:** Yes, it's plagiarism. You swapped synonyms ("overfit to" → "overfit on," "resulting in" → "leading to," "new examples" → "novel examples") but kept the structure. Correct: Restructure + cite, or quote if essential.

### Exercise 2: Fix the Paraphrase

**Original:** "Deep neural networks require significant computational resources during training." (Smith, 2019, p. 15)

**Bad paraphrase:** "Deep neural networks need major computational resources during training phase." [@smith2019]

**Good paraphrase:** "The training phase of deep neural networks is computationally intensive [@smith2019]." Or: "Computing requirements for training deep networks are substantial [@smith2019]."

### Exercise 3: Quote or Paraphrase?

**Situation:** You want to mention that Smith found a specific accuracy: "92.3% on ImageNet."

**Answer:** Quote if the exact number matters. "Smith et al. (2020) achieved 92.3% accuracy on ImageNet (p. 45)." (Quotes numbers without quotation marks, but cite source.)
